**Geometry** is a branch of [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics") concerned with properties of space such as the distance, shape, size, and relative position of figures. Geometry is, along with [arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic "Arithmetic"), one of the oldest branches of mathematics. A mathematician who works in the field of geometry is called a _[geometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_geometers "List of geometers")_. Until the 19th century, geometry was almost exclusively devoted to [Euclidean geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_geometry "Euclidean geometry"), which includes the notions of [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_\(geometry\) "Point (geometry)"), [line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_\(geometry\) "Line (geometry)"), [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_\(geometry\) "Plane (geometry)"), [distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance "Distance"), [angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle "Angle"), [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_\(mathematics\) "Surface (mathematics)"), and [curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve "Curve"), as fundamental concepts.

Originally developed to model the physical world, geometry has applications in almost all sciences, and also in art, [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture "Architecture"), and other activities that are related to graphics. Geometry also has applications in areas of mathematics that are apparently unrelated. For example, methods of algebraic geometry are fundamental in [Wiles's proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiles's_proof_of_Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem") of [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_Last_Theorem "Fermat's Last Theorem"), a problem that was stated in terms of [elementary arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_arithmetic "Elementary arithmetic"), and remained unsolved for several centuries.

During the 19th century, several discoveries enlarged dramatically the scope of geometry. One of the oldest such discoveries is [Carl Friedrich Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Friedrich_Gauss "Carl Friedrich Gauss")'s [Theorema Egregium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorema_Egregium "Theorema Egregium") ("remarkable theorem") that asserts roughly that the [Gaussian curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_curvature "Gaussian curvature") of a surface is independent from any specific [embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embedding "Embedding") in a [Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_space "Euclidean space"). This implies that surfaces can be studied _intrinsically_, that is, as stand-alone spaces, and has been expanded into the theory of [manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold "Manifold") and [Riemannian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_geometry "Riemannian geometry"). Later in the 19th century, it appeared that geometries without the [parallel postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_postulate "Parallel postulate") ([non-Euclidean geometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Euclidean_geometries "Non-Euclidean geometries")) can be developed without introducing any contradiction. The geometry that underlies [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity") is a famous application of non-Euclidean geometry.

Since the late 19th century, the scope of geometry has been greatly expanded, and the field has been split in many subfields that depend on the underlying methods—[differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry "Differential geometry"), [algebraic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_geometry "Algebraic geometry"), [computational geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_geometry "Computational geometry"), [algebraic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_topology "Algebraic topology"), [discrete geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_geometry "Discrete geometry") (also known as _combinatorial geometry_), etc.—or on the properties of Euclidean spaces that are disregarded—[projective geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_geometry "Projective geometry") that consider only alignment of points but not distance and parallelism, [affine geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_geometry "Affine geometry") that omits the concept of angle and distance, [finite geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_geometry "Finite geometry") that omits [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_\(mathematics\) "Continuity (mathematics)"), and others. This enlargement of the scope of geometry led to a change of meaning of the word "space", which originally referred to the three-dimensional [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space "Space") of the physical world and its [model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model "Model") provided by Euclidean geometry; presently a **geometric space**, or simply a _space_ is a [mathematical structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_structure "Mathematical structure") on which some geometry is defined.

### Space and Subspace
#### Points
> 🔗 Main article: [Point (geometry)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_\(geometry\) "Point (geometry)")

Points are generally considered fundamental objects for building geometry. They may be defined by the properties that they must have, as in Euclid's definition as "that which has no part",[43](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-EuclidAll-46) or in [synthetic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_geometry "Synthetic geometry"). In modern mathematics, they are generally defined as [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_\(set_theory\) "Element (set theory)") of a [set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_\(mathematics\) "Set (mathematics)") called [space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_\(mathematics\) "Space (mathematics)"), which is itself [axiomatically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatically "Axiomatically") defined.

With these modern definitions, every geometric shape is defined as a set of points; this is not the case in synthetic geometry, where a line is another fundamental object that is not viewed as the set of the points through which it passes.

However, there are modern geometries in which points are not primitive objects, or even without points.[44](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-47)[45](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-48) One of the oldest such geometries is [Whitehead's point-free geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitehead's_point-free_geometry "Whitehead's point-free geometry"), formulated by [Alfred North Whitehead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_North_Whitehead "Alfred North Whitehead") in 1919–1920.
#### Lines
> 🔗 Main article: [Line (geometry)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_\(geometry\) "Line (geometry)")

[Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid "Euclid") described a line as "breadthless length" which "lies equally with respect to the points on itself".[43](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-EuclidAll-46) In modern mathematics, given the multitude of geometries, the concept of a line is closely tied to the way the geometry is described. For instance, in [analytic geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_geometry "Analytic geometry"), a line in the plane is often defined as the set of points whose coordinates satisfy a given [linear equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_equation "Linear equation"),[46](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-49) but in a more abstract setting, such as [incidence geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incidence_geometry "Incidence geometry"), a line may be an independent object, distinct from the set of points which lie on it.[47](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-50) In differential geometry, a [geodesic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic "Geodesic") is a generalization of the notion of a line to [curved spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold "Manifold").[48](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-51)
#### Planes
> 🔗 Main article: [Euclidean plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane "Euclidean plane")

In Euclidean geometry a plane is a flat, two-dimensional surface that extends infinitely;[43](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-EuclidAll-46) the definitions for other types of geometries are generalizations of that. Planes are used in many areas of geometry. For instance, planes can be studied as a [topological surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_\(topology\) "Surface (topology)") without reference to distances or angles;[49](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-Munkres-52) it can be studied as an [affine space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_space "Affine space"), where collinearity and ratios can be studied but not distances;[50](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-53) it can be studied as the [complex plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_plane "Complex plane") using techniques of [complex analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_analysis "Complex analysis");[51](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-54) and so on.

#### Curves
> 🔗 Main article: [Curve (geometry)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve_\(geometry\) "Curve (geometry)")

A [curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve_\(geometry\) "Curve (geometry)") is a 1-dimensional object that may be straight (like a line) or not; curves in 2-dimensional space are called [plane curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_curve "Plane curve") and those in 3-dimensional space are called [space curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve "Space curve").[52](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-55)

In topology, a curve is defined by a function from an interval of the real numbers to another space.[49](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-Munkres-52) In differential geometry, the same definition is used, but the defining function is required to be differentiable.[53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-Carmo-56) Algebraic geometry studies [algebraic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_curve "Algebraic curve"), which are defined as [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties "Algebraic varieties") of [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_of_an_algebraic_variety "Dimension of an algebraic variety") one.[54](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-mumford-57)
#### Surfaces
> 🔗 Main article: [Surface (mathematics)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_\(mathematics\) "Surface (mathematics)")

A [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_\(mathematics\) "Surface (mathematics)") is a two-dimensional object, such as a sphere or paraboloid.[55](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-58) In [differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry "Differential geometry")[53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-Carmo-56) and [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology"),[49](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-Munkres-52) surfaces are described by two-dimensional 'patches' (or [neighborhoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_\(topology\) "Neighborhood (topology)")) that are assembled by [diffeomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism "Diffeomorphism") or [homeomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphism "Homeomorphism"), respectively. In algebraic geometry, surfaces are described by [polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation "Polynomial equation").[54](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-mumford-57)
#### Solids
> 🔗 Main article: [Solid geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_geometry "Solid geometry")

A [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_\(mathematics\) "Solid (mathematics)") is a three-dimensional object bounded by a closed surface; for example, a [ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_\(mathematics\) "Ball (mathematics)") is the volume bounded by a sphere.
#### Manifolds
> 🔗 Main article: [Manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold "Manifold")

A [manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold "Manifold") is a generalization of the concepts of curve and surface. In [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology "Topology"), a manifold is a [topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_space "Topological space") where every point has a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_\(topology\) "Neighborhood (topology)") that is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphism "Homeomorphism") to Euclidean space.[49](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-Munkres-52) In [differential geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry "Differential geometry"), a [differentiable manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_manifold "Differentiable manifold") is a space where each neighborhood is [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphism "Diffeomorphism") to Euclidean space.[53](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-Carmo-56)

Manifolds are used extensively in physics, including in [general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_relativity "General relativity") and [string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_theory "String theory").[56](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry#cite_note-59)

### Geometric Transformation 🤔

> 🔗 https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_transformation

In [mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics "Mathematics"), a **geometric transformation** is any [bijection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijection "Bijection") of a [set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_\(mathematics\) "Set (mathematics)") to itself (or to another such set) with some salient [geometrical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometry "Geometry") underpinning, such as preserving distances, [angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle "Angle"), or ratios (scale). More specifically, it is a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_\(mathematics\) "Function (mathematics)") whose [domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_of_a_function "Domain of a function") and [range](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_of_a_function "Range of a function") are sets of [points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_\(geometry\) "Point (geometry)") – most often a [real coordinate space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_coordinate_space "Real coordinate space"), $\mathbb {R} ^{2}$ or $\mathbb {R} ^{3}$ – such that the function is bijective so that its [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_function "Inverse function") exists. The study of geometry may be approached by the study of these transformations, such as in [transformation geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_geometry "Transformation geometry").
#### Classification & Inclusion Hierarchy
Geometric transformations can be classified by the dimension of their operand sets (thus distinguishing between, say, planar transformations and spatial transformations). They can also be classified according to the properties they preserve:
- [Displacements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_\(vector\) "Displacement (vector)") preserve [distances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance) and [oriented angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle "Angle") (e.g., [translations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_\(geometry\) "Translation (geometry)"));
- [Isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometry "Isometry") preserve angles and distances (e.g., [Euclidean transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_transformation "Euclidean transformation"));
- [Similarities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similarity_\(geometry\) "Similarity (geometry)") preserve angles and ratios between distances (e.g., resizing);
- [Affine transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_transformation "Affine transformation") preserve [parallelism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_\(geometry\) "Parallel (geometry)") (e.g., [scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_\(geometry\) "Scaling (geometry)"), [shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_mapping "Shear mapping"));
- [Projective transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation "Projective transformation") preserve [collinearity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinearity "Collinearity");
Each of these classes contains the previous one.
